Abstract
·Philipp Schaer1 ·
The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced a paradigm shift in Information Retrieval (IR),
moving beyond conventional keyword queries and ranked result lists. LLMs now play a critical role in the evolution of
IR technologies and introduce new interaction forms like Retrieval-Augmented Generation, which is a more dynamic and
interactive retrieval process that integrates various aspects of Information Access, like Question Answering, into the dialog
between a searcher and the search engine. We explore the multi-faceted impact of LLMs on IR, particularly in three
distinct layers where they have become an integral part of the retrieval process, namely the retrieval system and processing
pipeline that can make use of a richer semantic representation using advanced language models, the interaction layer, and
the broader IR ecosystem. For the latter, we focus on evaluation issues as well as bias, fairness, and ethical concerns. We
also highlight some recent cases of using LLMs in the medical domain to demonstrate the impact on one specific domain.
1 Introduction
The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs)
has significantly transformed the landscape of Information
Access (IA) and Information Retrieval (IR) in particular.
Traditionally, IR has been centered around search engines
that rank and retrieve documents based on keyword match
ing and statistical models. However, the rise of LLMs has
introduced a paradigm shift, moving beyond conventional
ranked result lists. LLMs now play a critical role in the
evolution of IR technologies and introduce new interaction
forms like Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), which
is a more dynamic and interactive retrieval process that inte
grates various aspects of IA like Question Answering (QA)
into the dialog between a searcher and the search engine.

This paper explores the multi-faceted impact of LLMs
on IR, particularly in three distinct layers where they have
become an integral part of the retrieval process.
First, LLMs contribute directly to the retrieval system
and the processing pipeline by enhancing indexing, gener
ating enriched word embeddings, or supporting query and
document expansion techniques (see Sect. 2). These im
provements leverage the broad “world knowledge” encap
sulated within LLMs with the aim to increase search effec
tiveness. LLM-enhanced indexing techniques allow search
engines to build more semantically aware representations
of text, leading to more contextually relevant retrieval re
sults. Furthermore, LLMs assist in query understanding by
reformulating queries to bridge the gap between user intent
and indexed information.
Second, LLMs are an important part of the interaction
layer and are reshaping the human-computer dialogue with
search systems (see Sect. 3). Unlike traditional keyword
based search models, LLM-supported conversational in
terfaces facilitate a more interactive and iterative search
experience. This transition introduces new interaction
paradigms, shifting the focus from static ranked result lists
to a dialogue-based retrieval approach. The ability of LLMs
to interpret complex queries, provide nuanced responses,
and adapt to user feedback in real-time is transforming
search into a more intuitive and efficient process.

Finally, LLMs serve as meta-tools or frameworks that
enhance the broader IR ecosystem (see Sect. 4). They as
sist in the synthetic creation of IR test collections and refine
relevance assessment methodologies but can also be used
to improve the realism of user simulations. By enabling
more sophisticated test environments, LLMs contribute to
advancing IR research, allowing for the evaluation of new
retrieval techniques in increasingly complex and realistic
settings. Their role in benchmarking and assessing retrieval
performance is becoming more present and important [23],
providing deeper insights into system behavior and effec
tiveness. Furthermore, LLMs facilitate the automated as

on human annotators, and accelerating the development of
more robust retrieval models. However, as these models re

challenges, such as biases, fairness concerns, and the inter

main crucial in ensuring ethical and unbiased information

The influence of LLMs on IR is not merely a supple
mentary enhancement but a fundamental shift in how infor
mation access is carried out. The integration of LLMs into
retrieval pipelines, user interfaces, and evaluation frame
works has the potential to change our understanding of the
field and to enable new system designs where information
retrieval is more intelligent, contextual, and interactive [51].
This is demonstrated by a brief overview of recent use cases
(see Sect. 5).
In this overview paper, we focus on the innovative appli
cations and solutions that LLM-enhanced IR systems offer,
as well as unresolved challenges and limitations. By ex
amining the evolving role of LLMs, we aim to provide
a comprehensive perspective on how these models shape
the future of information access and retrieval. The contin
uous development of LLM-integrated IR systems presents
opportunities for more efficient, accurate, and user-centric
information access, opening new research directions and
practical implementations that redefine the boundaries of
traditional IR systems.
2 ASystem-orientedPerspectiveOn the
Interplay of IR and LLMs
In the following, we review the current state of the art
and challenges ahead from a system-oriented perspective
on the interplay of Information Retrieval (IR) technology
and Large Language Models (LLM). While the topic is
complex, intertwined, and involves plenty of open research
directions, we follow a threefold topical (split of 1) works
where IR supports the LLM ((cf. Sect. 2.1), 2) vice versa,
the LLM supports the IR component ((cf. Sect. 2.2), and 3)
another topical cluster where LLM is not an active part of
the IR system but instead supports it implicitly or is used
to evaluate it (cf. Sect. 2.3). The following text represents
a condensed review of recent work and efforts, and we can
only scratch the tip of the iceberg in this overview article.
We refer the interested reader to several recent perspec
tive articles and overview reports for further reading on the
matter [2, 3, 26, 79].
2.1 IR-enhanced LLMs
These days, the most popular application of IR in support of
LLMsis Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) [37]. The
underlying principle of RAG implies evidence grounded in
a ranking that is provided to the LLMs to generate a natural
language answer in response to a user’s prompt or query.
In this case, the IR component is located at an intermediate
stage in a data pipeline rather than at the interface level. The
users of a RAG-based system would not interact directly
with the ranking or outputs of the IR system but interact
with the generated text answer inferred from the ranking.
LLMs are known for generating or hallucinating false
information sometimes [32]. RAG-based systems provide
more accurate and factual answers than LLMs without re
trieval-based guidance. The IR component provides a rank
ing of information items, e.g., text passages, as a counter
measure to guide the LLM, thus avoiding hallucinations
[57]. Likewise, it helps the LLMs to stay updated with re
cent information without the need for retraining, which can
have a considerable impact on the costs of running these
kinds of systems. Currently, it is more cost-efficient to keep
an index updated than retraining an LLM [38].
State-of-the-art RAG-based systems rely on vector
databases where each information item is represented by an
embedding vector [10]. The retrieved ranked list of items
is usually implemented with nearest neighbor search meth
ods. In this context, compromises have to be made between
Exact Nearest Neighbors (ENN) and Approximate Nearest
Neighbors (ANN) search methods [52]. ENN methods de
liver precise rankings but are costly in terms of memory
bandwidth, while ANN methods allow faster retrieval at
the cost of less effective rankings. In other words, ENN
based rankings allow more accurate generated answers
with fewer items retrieved, whereas ANN methods need
to retrieve more items to lay the foundation of generated
answers with comparable quality. In the end, tradeoffs have
to be made between the quality of the generated outputs
and the efficiency of the retrieval and generation steps,
which are also highly influenced by the available hardware
resources.

RAG-based generated answers allow tailored and con

rankings alone normally cannot provide. RAG-based sys

search fields of Conversational Search (ConvSearch) and
Information Seeking, where there is a sequence of inter
actions between the user and the system [66]. ConvSearch
systems envision interactive conversations that help users
fulfill their information-seeking or transactional tasks. Open
topics are, among others, proactive search actions by the
conversational agent or how system answers shall be pro
vided, e.g., with different modalities like rankings or con

One of the major challenges remains the efficient and
automated evaluation of machine-generated answers to user
queries. While in a typical ad hoc retrieval experiment, the
number of correct answers, i.e., relevant items, is fixed,
there is, in principle, an infinite number of possible an
swers generated by an LLM. Recent works propose semi
automated human-in-the-loop approaches [49] or fully au
tomated methods [19] with other LLMs than those evalu
ated. Fundamental to most of these approaches is the iden
tification of so-called information nuggets—small, concise
pieces of information that are valuable or significant on
their own.
2.2 LLM-enhanced IR
In this case, the LLM supports the retrieval method in re
trieving information items. From a user-oriented perspec
tive, the system returns a ranked list as a response to a nat
ural language query, i.e., the final system output to be eval
uated is a list of ranked items, e.g., text passages. Gen
erally, language models have been integral to many tradi
tional ranking methods [14]. However, the introduction of
the transformer architecture and its attention mechanism
[70], and BERT in particular [18], led to substantial effec
tiveness improvements lately. A typical multi-stage pipeline
is made with a cost-efficient, lexical, first-stage ranker that
is followed by a more computationally costly second-stage
reranker that is based on a fine-tuned LLM or its embed
dings [41]. Current state-of-the-art approaches leverage in
struction-tuned LLMs, often as rerankers in zero-shot set
tings, where no fine-tuning of the language model is re
quired due to the extensive world knowledge that is inherent
in these kinds of LLMs [61].
Lately, the idea of Generative Retrieval [50] has estab
lished a paradigm shift of the typical index-then-retrieve
pipeline, and instead, leverages the LLM to generate the
identifier of the relevant item in response to a user query.
There is no inverted index in the traditional sense, but
the generative model infers the required information from
pre-trained knowledge. Recent works on this topic include
countermeasures to the false pruning problem by term set
generation [81], or novel optimization and decoding ap
proaches [78].
2.3 Other UseCases ofLLMsin IR
Besides the tight integration of IR and LLMs, instruction
tuned LLMs have been exploited to support the IR sys
tem and the experimental setup, recently. One of the most
popular research topics is the automated generation of rel
evance labels with the help of LLMs. Basically, the model
is provided with the information need or query, the infor
mation item, e.g., a text passage, and the prompt to make
a judgment about the relevance [65]. Beyond relevance la
bels, other work proposes generating an entire synthetic test
collection, including both topics and corresponding labels
[54]. In Sect. 4, we provide a more in-depth discussion of
the state-of-the-art evaluation of IR systems with LLMs.
In addition to relevance labels, LLMs also offer great
potential for more realistic user modeling as part of simula
tions [25, 80]. Conventional user simulations followed rule
based approaches that have a high degree of interpretability
but often lack fidelity with real user behavior. To address
this shortcoming, recent work proposed modeling each user
interaction with an instruction-tuned LLM [80], suggest
ing a higher level of fidelity. In this context, several works
also use instruction-tuned LLMs to generate queries [1]that
align with real user queries reasonably well.
Other topics include training data generation with in
struction-tuned LLMs to fine-tune dense-retrievers [34]or
using them as a preprocessing component, e.g., for stem
ming [73] or document expansion [47].
3 UserPerspective
The integration of LLMs into IR systems significantly
changes the user experience, introducing new interaction
paradigms while addressing critical challenges such as text
simplification and user trust. We are now at a stage where,
with LLMs, we can enrich user interaction in a way that
solves central problems and helps fulfill visions for search
systems formulated in the past [72]. For instance, users can
formulate queries/prompts in natural language and provide

“compromised information need” described by [63], where

shift enables more accurate expression of intent, bridg

Furthermore, LLMs facilitate multimodal and multilingual
interactions, advancing toward White’s vision [74] of search
systems as personalized assistants capable of understanding
user behavior, context, and document relevance. Beyond
traditional information retrieval, LLMs extend support to
exploratory search tasks, such as synthesizing information,
conducting comparisons, and integrating information seek
ing with application activities central to complex search
behavior as outlined by [48].
3.1 Interaction Paradigms
A critical challenge lies in designing systems that allow
LLMstoenableintuitive interaction while addressing inher
ent complexities. One emerging paradigm is conversational
search, where users engage in dynamic, iterative dialogues
with the system to refine queries, clarify ambiguities, and
navigate information needs [67]. This contrasts with tra
ditional keyword-based retrieval, as LLMs allow users to
express queries in complete sentences or paragraphs and to
refine queries through dialogue, thereby reducing cognitive
load and improving precision. Direct question answering
further streamlines the process, enabling systems to synthe
size answers from multiple sources rather than merely re
turning documents or single sentences extracted from these
documents. Users also gain precise control through instruc
tion-following, issuing commands like “Summarize this ar
ticle” or “Compare these two arguments”, thereby expand
ing the scope of IR systems to encompass complex infor
mation processing tasks or, as White [75] put it, advancing
the task frontier.
Adaptability is another aspect, as LLMs can adapt inter
actions to user preferences, resolve ambiguities through
clarification dialogues, and structure responses to reflect
multiple interpretations of vague queries. Contextual aware
ness plays a key role. While current systems often require
explicit inclusion of background information in prompts,
future advancements aim to implicitly integrate context
through conversation history, user profiles, or situational
awareness, creating seamless and adaptive interactions [68].
3.2 TextTransformation
LLMs excel at transforming complex texts into accessi
ble formats, broadening information accessibility. Lexical
simplification replaces technical or obscure terms with
simpler synonyms, assisting users with limited literacy or
domain knowledge. Syntactic simplification restructures
complicated sentences into shorter, clearer units, enhancing
readability [22]. Content simplification strips non-essen
tial details, reducing cognitive load and emphasizing core
ideas. Summarization capabilities condense lengthy texts
or even aggregated search results-into concise overviews,
enabling quick comprehension. Cross-language interactions
break down linguistic barriers by automatically translating
queries and documents, while structured transformations
convert prose into tables or other formats, facilitating data
interpretation. Together, these techniques empower users
to engage with information in ways that align with their
unique needs and capabilities. Regarding the amount of
information, summarization techniques can help reduce in
formation overload, a problem traditionally regarded as re
sulting from an overwhelming number of documents, either
in a collection or a result set [8]. However, LLMs can also
inadvertently amplify information overload by generating
excessively verbose or redundant content, overwhelming
users with unnecessary details even when summarization
is applied. The ease of producing large volumes of text
through LLMs might lead to an overabundance of informa
tion, requiring users to sift through more material to discern
relevance. For example, repetitive outputs in conversational
interactions can increase cognitive load despite the intent
to clarify. Balancing conciseness with comprehensiveness
thus becomes critical to ensure LLMs alleviate rather than
exacerbate information overload [35].
3.3 UserTrust
A significant challenge is building and maintaining user
trust in LLM-based IR systems [20]. A critical first step
is improving explainability: users require transparent ratio
nales for system responses to counteract the inherent “black
box” nature of LLMs. Explanations can range from high
lighting relevant passages in source documents to providing
a rationale for the system’s reasoning process. A challenge
is not to produce text that convinces the user and does
not really explain the system’s reasoning. Equally critical
is source attribution, which anchors responses in verifiable
references, allowing users to evaluate credibility, a neces
sity given the propensity of LLMs to hallucinate unfounded
claims [31]. Equipping systems to handle uncertainty fur
ther mitigates risks, particularly in high-stakes domains
such as healthcare [39]. By signaling confidence levels,
presenting alternative interpretations, or directing users to
authoritative sources, systems can navigate errors grace
fully while preserving trust. Together, these strategies form
a framework for aligning LLM outputs with user expecta
tions of reliability and transparency. Trustworthiness also
declines when other ethical issues like induced bias or un
fairness become apparent, for this we refer to Sect. 4.2.

3.4 UserDiversity
Designing inclusive LLM-driven IR systems demands care
ful attention to user diversity, ensuring accessibility across
cultural, cognitive, and contextual dimensions. Cultural sen
sitivity is important, requiring systems to recognize di
verse norms and values while mitigating biases embed
ded in training data [27]. Techniques like Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) [53] offer pathways to align outputs
with culturally appropriate frameworks. Personalization fur
ther enhances inclusivity by tailoring language, detail, and
formats to individual needs-such as adapting responses to
a user’s expertise level or information goals-though this
must be balanced against privacy concerns to avoid over
reliance on personal data [43]. Strategic summarization,
hierarchical presentation of key points, and intuitive nav
igation mechanisms help users filter and prioritize content
efficiently. By integrating these approaches, IR systems can
accommodate heterogeneous user backgrounds and needs,
reducing cognitive barriers while fostering equitable access
to information.
4 LLMsShapingaNew IR Ecosystem
The use of Generative Artificial Intelligence in general and
LLMs in particular introduces new opportunities that also
come with several challenges and caveats. In the following,
we review the state of the art and challenges ahead with
a special focus on the evaluation of the experimental setup
in Sect. 4.1, as well as bias, fairness, and ethics in Sect. 4.2.
4.1 Evaluation
In alignment with the system-oriented perspectives of Chap
ter Sect. 2, LLMs can either be used as a means of support
ing the evaluation of IR systems, or vice versa, the LLM it
self is the evaluation target, e.g., as part of a conversational
agent facilitating the information access. For this reason,
the following is divided into two parts, where Sect. 4.1.1
gives an overview of current efforts about the use of LLM
enhanced evaluations of IR systems, and Sect. 4.1.2 fo
cuses on the evaluation of conversational agents, distilling
the outcomes of a dedicated Dagstuhl workshop [7].
4.1.1 LLM-enhanced Evaluation of IR Systems
When using LLM-based technologies for evaluating the ef
fectiveness of IR systems, the core question boils down to
whether LLMs can correctly infer the relevance of a given
piece of text in correspondence to an underlying infor
mation need usually expressed by a topic. Therefore, sev
eral works assess whether LLM-based relevance judgments
align with those made intellectually by humans.
Anextensive study by Microsoft suggests that LLMs can
provide reasonable judgments that are on par with crowd as
sessors [65]. Concerns have been raised, however, that these
kinds of labels are not good enough to identify exception
ally well-performing systems but rather help to draw the
line between reasonable and low-performing systems [58].
Another work by Takehi et al. [62] suggests that LLM
based relevance judgments are overly optimistic with too
many documents judged as relevant, i.e., as false positives.
Especially if the same underlying LLM is used for both
the relevance assessment and the ranking, the experimental
setup will run into circularities that inevitably may bias the
overall outcome with LLM-based label generators that pre
fer ranking models that rely on LLMs of the same model
family. In this context, the reader might be inclined to the
mental image of the ouroboros—a snake biting its own
tail—analogously to an LLM evaluating its own ranking
preferences with its own judgments. Of course, a differ
ent, preferably better, LLM should be used to evaluate an
LLM-based retrieval approach. However, this cross-model
evaluation setup has its limitations, once the best-perform
ing model, i.e., the one with the most world knowledge, is
used as a ranker. What is a good evaluator in this case? For
a more in-depth discussion, we refer the reader to Balog
et al. [5].
Instead of fully relying on LLMs to make the relevance
judgments, semi-automated human-in-the-loop approaches
have been proposed. As one of the very first conceptual
approaches, Faggioli et al. [23] propose hybrid approaches
that involve both human as well as LLM-based judgments
to different extents. In ongoing community efforts, the dis
cussed challenges and others are currently addressed; for
instance, as part of the LLM4Eval workshop series [54, 55]
or as part of the TREC Retrieval Augmented Generation
Task [69].
4.1.2 Evaluation of LLM-enhanced Conversational Agents
As LLM-based systems allow for conversational informa
tion access, they pose specific challenges for their eval
uation. A recent Dagstuhl Perspectives Workshop “Con
versational Agents: A Framework for Evaluation (CAFE)”
addressed this issue by developing a general model for the
evaluation of conversational information access (CONIAC)
systems. The following text represents a condensed version
of the workshop findings. We refer the interested reader to
the extended workshop report for more detailed discussions
and additional references [7].
The framework starts from the assumption that a CO
NIAC system will be able to (i) interact with users more
naturally and seamlessly, (ii) guide a user through the pro

The CAFE framework distinguishes six major elements
of an evaluation:
StakeholderGoals Stakeholders of a CONIAC system may
have diverse goals that might or might not be directly ac
cessible to system designers or evaluators and must often be
implicitly inferred in evaluation. CONIAC systems might
also have multiple goals ranging from end users having (in
)direct information needs, to platforms deploying CONIAC
systems interested in content usage, user engagement, im
pression generation, user retention, to name a few.
Tasks CONIAC involves tasks characterized by an informa
tion need, human involvement, goal orientation, and mixed
initiative between the user and the system. While some tasks
benefit from the introduction of a conversationally compe
tent system, others may not, depending on the complexity
of the task.
User Aspects When developing an evaluation framework
for CONIAC systems, it is crucial to consider user-spe
cific aspects, such as preferences, specialized needs, exper
tise types, and background characteristics, which may make
conversational systems more beneficial than non-conversa
tional alternatives.
Criteria The scope of evaluation can range from single-turn
interactions to entire conversations and long-term system
usage, each requiring different criteria for assessment. Ad
ditionally, the temporal dimension, which examines how the
system’s performance changes over time, is a critical factor
that intersects with both stationary and dynamic properties,
making it essential for a comprehensive evaluation.
Methodology In addition to the standard distinction of
user-focused and system-focused methodologies, the eval
uation framework categorizes evaluation methodologies
also according to the employed time model—a dimension
especially relevant for CONIAC. This dimension ranges
from stationary methodologies like single-interaction ex
periments to methodologies like controlled lab studies that
allow for continuous measurements, for example, physio
logical ones.
Measures reflect the system’s ability to provide accurate,
relevant, and timely information during interactions. These
include objective measures of effectiveness to subjective
notions of user satisfaction like self-reported satisfaction.
By incorporating both objective effectiveness measures and
subjective self-reported satisfaction, evaluators can better
understand the system’s strengths and areas for improve
ment.
When designing an evaluation, the first step is to iden
tify the stakeholders and the goals that need to be addressed.
Based on the goals, the user tasks to be studied in the eval
uation have to be defined, and the user aspects must be con
sidered. The central element of an evaluation is the criteria
to be focused on, which are determined by the stakeholder
goals. Roughly speaking, we can distinguish between user
centric and system-centric criteria, and the chosen crite
ria restrict the range of possible evaluation methods (i.e.,
any user-centric criterion requires the involvement of actual
users in the evaluation procedure). Finally, for each quanti
tative criterion, an appropriate measure has to be defined.
4.2 Bias,Fairness, and EthicalConcerns
The intersection of LLMs and IR presents both opportuni
ties and challenges, particularly regarding bias, fairness, and
ethical concerns. While LLMs have apparently improved
the retrieval performance of many systems, they also in
troduce new forms of bias and ethical risks that have to
be carefully addressed. Due to the nature of their training
data, LLMs inherently amplify biases, which manifest at
various stages of the IR pipeline, including embedding rep
resentations, document ranking, and query interpretation.
However, LLMs can also serve as tools to mitigate bias,
either by identifying and quantifying it or by re-ranking
search results to improve fairness.
In addition to the positive effects already mentioned,
LLMs can also introduce bias into the search process. Bias
is an inherent part of all LLMs as they were trained on
potentially biased datasets, and although a huge amount
of work is done to de-bias LLMs in their training phase,
the issue still remains. LLMs in IR can introduce bias at
different levels, like on the embedding level or in rank
ing and re-ranking processes. Bias introduces unfairness.
Biased search systems can produce biased and, therefore,
unfair results. This is not new, and the IR community has
ongoing hands-on campaigns like the TREC Fair Ranking
track from 2019–2022 or the NTCIR Fair Web Task [56].
However, these shared task campaigns do not yet include
any LLM-based search scenarios. The whole issue is stuck
in traditional ad-hoc retrieval settings and does not incor
porate current RAG-based scenarios.
In the following, we will give a brief overview of current
topics with respect to bias, fairness, and ethical concerns
along the three main bias sources and the most promising
mitigation strategies as summarized in Table 1. For a more
extensive overview on biases in IR systems, we would like
to point the reader to two recent surveys [4, 16].

4.2.1 Sources of Bias and Unfairness
As described earlier, the rise of LLMs has changed the
pipeline and system design of IR systems. In these new
LLM-based IR systems, three stages can be identified as
bias and unfairness sources: data collection, model devel
opment, and result evaluation (see Table 1).
Newdatasources. LLMs generate content for IR as new
data sources and training material [12]. Unlike traditional
content sources that (re-)use professional or user-generated
content, LLMs can produce vast amounts of text at scale.
This can have an influence on the text distributions in the
data, introducing new biases like source bias or factual
ity bias, as LLMs have the tendency to create non-factual
or “hallucinated” content that is again favored by neural
matching techniques in contrast to human-authored content
[17].
LLM-based IR models. LLMs introduce randomness
into the retrieval process, like in the results generation step
of a RAG system [84].Daietal. [16] categorize these in
consistency biases into four groups: position bias, popular
ity bias, input-hallucination bias, and context-hallucination
bias. Here, LLMs have been described as preferring docu
ments from specific input positions or giving higher rank
ings for popular items (also known as the Matthew effect).
Result evaluation. Human relevance judgments play an
essential role in the evaluation of IR systems. However, hu
man evaluations are challenging and expensive and intro
duce reproducibility issues. LLMs offer new possibilities
for conducting evaluations without human evaluation. This
step, while useful and practical, is highly debated in the
IR community [58] asLLMshavebeenshown tobebi
ased with respect to position, style, or a specific family of
LLMs, which makes methods like BARTScore favor texts
produced by their underlying model [82].

beled datasets [60], but these datasets do not exist for many
domains. Here, strategies like data augmentation and data
filtering are applied [13]. By utilizing recent LLMs, the
need for fine-tuning and specific datasets can be omitted.
Haak et al. [28] have shown that LLMs can be used to
quantify the bias intensity by producing a score based on
many pairwise relative bias assessments that can be con
ducted with the help of LLMs. The latter can be used to
improve the ability of RAG systems to correct themselves
[77].
Another mitigation strategy is to correct distortions in
the data by applying rebalancing, regularization, or tuning
the prompting.
4.2.3 Ethical Concerns
Ethical issues besides the previously discussed bias and
fairness issues emerge when LLMs are part of IR systems,
like misinformation and hallucination or privacy and data
leakage.
An imminent feature of LLMs is their ability to generate
highly readable output that sounds plausible, although this
output can still be incorrect or misleading. These outputs
are called “hallucinations.” Users might retrieve pseudo
factual data or rely on citations, dates, or misrepresented
content. The level of trust users show against (LLM-en
hanced) search engines is questionable [29].
Privacy issues and data leakage are situations where
training data might include sensitive or personally iden
tifiable information, and LLMs include this information in
the output. Retrieving or exposing private user data vio
lates ethical and sometimes legal standards (e.g., GDPR)
but can also harm the user trust in LLM-based systems (see
Sect. 3.3). A query about a public figure might return pri
vate details sourced from improperly filtered training data
[59].
Other concerns are related to the lack of transparency
and explainability of LLM-generated content, issues related
to intellectual property and copyright violations, and user
manipulation and an over-reliance on LLMs that can reduce
critical thinking or make users vulnerable to manipulation
(intentional or not). All of these issues are too broad to
be covered here, but we would like to point the readers
to conferences like ACM FAccT or the recent CLEF lab
ELOQUENT [33] that cover a lot of these very pressing
issues of LLMs both on a technical level and their societal
impacts.

5 UseCases
LLMs have found applications in diverse fields that show
case their ability to improve information access and deci
sion-making processes. As the list of current examples and
use cases is long and heterogeneous, we start by outlining
some general applications and focusing only on one do
main, the medical applications of LLMs. Here, LLMs have
huge potential to revolutionize healthcare and improve pa
tient outcomes.
5.1 General Applications
Prominent non-medical applications include:
ContentGeneration.LLMsarewidelyusedtodraftarti
cles, create marketing copy, and generate creative content
across industries, typically using the RAG architecture,
which grounds the text in evidence before writing [37].
Education and Research. LLMs assist students and
researchers in accessing relevant academic literature,
summarizing papers, and generating reports. Tools like
ChatGPT and Claude have become integral in academic
workflows, as LLMs can predict searchers preferences
[65]. LLM-based academic copilots first retrieve and
rank relevant papers (e.g., instruction-tuned rerankers)
before summarizing them for the learner [40].
Legal Assistance. In the legal domain, LLMs are used
to retrieve precedents, summarize legal documents, and
provide insights into case law, enabling lawyers to save
time and improve accuracy. Modern case-law assistants
rely on legal case retrieval engines that fetch precedents
and feed them to the LLM for drafting [76].
Customer Support. Businesses employ LLMs to power
chatbots and virtual assistants, providing personalized
and timely responses to customer queries. Enterprise
chatbots usually call an internal search API or vector in
dex to retrieve frequently asked questions (FAQs) before
generating personalized replies [36].
Among these, the medical domain presents both the po
tential and challenges of using LLMs in IR systems.
5.2 MedicalApplicationsofLLMs
The integration of LLMs into medicine presents a transfor
mative yet complex landscape. While offering unique op
portunities to enhance information access, streamline work
flows, and accelerate research, their application in health
care necessitates careful consideration of inherent chal
lenges and ethical implications. LLMs hold the potential
to improve clinician access to information by summariz
ing complex clinical guidelines and literature [9], aid re
searchers in evidence synthesis, and drug discovery, and
empower patients through conversational interfaces for vir
tual consultations and accessible health guidance. Further
more, LLMs can advance personalized medicine by ana
lyzing multimodal patient data, including genomic profiles
and electronic health records (EHRs), to inform tailored
treatment plans [6].
Despite these advancements, significant challenges re
main. The risk of LLMs generating inaccurate or fabricated
medical information (“hallucinations”) poses critical safety
concerns in diagnosis and treatment recommendations [71].
Biases embedded in training data-such as underrepresenta
tion of rare diseases or minority populations-can propagate
discriminatory outcomes. Privacy mandates require special
ized architectures to anonymize sensitive patient data while
maintaining clinical utility, often necessitating Health In
surance Portability and Accountability Act(HIPAA)-com
pliant LLMs. The unclear decision-making processes of
LLMs hinder interpretability, complicating trust-building
with clinicians [83]. Practical integration challenges persist,
including alignment with structured EHR formats like Fast
Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) and real-time
retrieval from dynamic clinical databases. Ethical dilemmas
surrounding accountability for errors and the redefinition of
clinician-patient relationships further underscore the need
for cautious implementation [11].
Patient-centered applications demonstrate both prom
ise and pitfalls. LLMs streamline administrative tasks by
auto-generating discharge summaries and symptom-check
ing chatbots [21]. However, these systems must reconcile
free-text generation with structured EHR templates, re
quiring fine-tuning on standard medical ontologies. RAG
mitigates hallucinations by grounding outputs in patient
specific data, though seamless integration with real-time
lab results remains technically demanding. Privacy remains
paramount, as systems must anonymize outputs to com
ply with regulations like HIPAA while retaining medical
relevance-a balance achieved through tokenization tech
niques and synthetic data generation [64].
In clinical decision support, LLMs synthesize unstruc
tured physician notes, imaging reports, and pharmacologi
cal data to propose differential diagnoses. Multimodal sys
tems cross-reference medical guidelines with patient histo
ries to suggest evidence-based therapies [6, 24]. Challenges
include maintaining temporal relevance amid rapidly evolv
ing medical knowledge and filtering conflicting studies dur
ing retrieval [46]. Hybrid frameworks that combine LLM
generated insights with clinician oversight are emerging to
balance automation with human accountability [83].
For medical research, LLMs accelerate systematic re
views by extracting key findings from PubMed’s 1 million+
annual publications. Synthetic data generation enables pri
vacy-preserving model training using realistic EHR simu
lations [30], while cross-lingual retrieval democratizes ac

Future directions emphasize medical-specific LLMs

6 Conclusion
LLMs enhance and replace many specialized functions that
have been explored over decades of IR research. Their
greatest potential in IR lies in serving as intermediaries
between users and retrieval backends, enabling a shift from
traditional query-based interactions to more natural, con
versational exchanges. To fully leverage this potential, user
centric evaluation methods will become increasingly impor
tant. However, LLMs also introduce new challenges, such
as hallucinations, as well as longstanding AI concerns re
lated to bias, fairness, and ethical implications.
The 1992 Charter of the German GI-IR group defines
IR as addressing vague queries and uncertain knowledge
within information systems, a definition that remains rele
vant today. However, there is a growing need to shift focus
away from systems and toward user-centric perspectives.
At the same time, incorporating vagueness and uncertainty
into LLM-based IR approaches remains an open research
challenge.
